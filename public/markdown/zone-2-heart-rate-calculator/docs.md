## What the Zone 2 Heart Rate Calculator Does

Type in your age, and the calculator estimates your maximum heart rate using the **Tanaka formula**, then applies it to the 60–70% range that defines Zone 2 training heart rate. Add your resting heart rate, and it switches to the **Karvonen method** for a more individualized number.

The output is simple: a lower and upper heart rate boundary in beats per minute (BPM). Stay within that window during your cardio session, and you are training in the aerobic zone, which is built for endurance, fat metabolism, and long-term heart health.

If you want the full background on why this zone matters, our Zone 2 Heart Rate Guide covers the physiology in depth. This page focuses on the number itself: how it is calculated, what it means, and how to use it correctly.

## Zone 2 Heart Rate Formulas

Two formulas do the heavy lifting here. Most calculators, including this one, combine them.

### 1. Maximum Heart Rate (Tanaka Formula)

$$ \text{Maximum Heart Rate} = 208 - (0.7 \times \text{Age}) $$

This formula comes from a 2001 meta-analysis of 351 studies covering more than 18,000 people, published in the ***Journal of the American College of Cardiology***. It replaced the older "220 minus age" rule, which was never based on a formal study and tends to overestimate max heart rate in older adults.

The Tanaka equation still carries a margin of error of about ±10 bpm, because genetics, training history, and even medication affect an individual max heart rate. It is a strong estimate, not a lab result.

### 2. Zone 2 Heart Rate Range

$$ \text{Zone 2 Lower Bound} = \text{Max HR} \times 0.60 $$

$$ \text{Zone 2 Upper Bound} = \text{Max HR} \times 0.70 $$

Zone 2 represents **60–70% of maximum heart rate** within the standard five-zone training model favored by coaches, sports scientists, and wearable brands. This intensity roughly corresponds to the **first lactate threshold (LT1)**, where the body clears lactate at the same rate it produces it and relies mostly on fat for fuel.

### 3. Karvonen Formula (Heart Rate Reserve Method)

$$ \text{Heart Rate Reserve (HRR)} = \text{Max HR} - \text{Resting HR} $$

$$ \text{Target Heart Rate} = (\text{HRR} \times \text{Intensity}\%) + \text{Resting HR} $$

Developed by Finnish physiologist **Martti Karvonen in 1957**, this method accounts for your fitness level, not just your age. Two 40-year-olds with the same max heart rate can have very different Zone 2 ranges if one has a resting heart rate of 50 bpm and the other has 75 bpm.

The Karvonen method is the one the [**American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM)**](https://acsm.org/) recommends when resting heart rate data is available, precisely because it personalizes the number instead of treating everyone born in the same year as identical.

## How to Calculate Zone 2 Heart Rate (Step-by-Step)

You can do this by hand in under a minute. Here is the step-by-step walkthrough:

**Step 1: Find your estimated max heart rate.** Subtract 0.7 times your age from 208. For example, a 35-year-old gets $208 - (0.7 \times 35) = 183.5$, rounded to **184 BPM**.

**Step 2: Multiply by 0.60 and 0.70.** $184 \times 0.60 = 110\text{ BPM}$ and $184 \times 0.70 = 129\text{ BPM}$. That 35-year-old's Zone 2 range is roughly **110–129 BPM**.

**Step 3 (Optional, for more accuracy): Add resting heart rate.** If that same person has a resting heart rate of 60 BPM, their heart rate reserve is $184 - 60 = 124$. Zone 2 on the Karvonen scale (50–60% HRR) works out to: $(124 \times 0.50) + 60 = \mathbf{122\text{ BPM}}$ and $(124 \times 0.60) + 60 = \mathbf{134\text{ BPM}}$. Notice the Karvonen number runs slightly higher because it rewards better cardiovascular fitness.

**Step 4: Confirm with the Talk Test.** Formulas get you in the right neighborhood. Your breathing confirms you have arrived. If you can hold a full conversation without gasping between sentences, you are in Zone 2. If you can only manage single words, dial it back.

## Interpreting Your Zone 2 Heart Rate (5 Key Rules)

A number on a screen does not train you. Knowing what to do with it does:

**It should feel almost too easy.** Exercise physiologist **Iñigo San-Millán**, whose research helped popularize this training zone, defines it as the highest effort you can sustain while keeping blood lactate below roughly 2 mmol/L. In practice, that means an effort most people instinctively think is "too slow to count."

**Your breathing stays through your nose, mostly.** Once you need your mouth to keep up with oxygen demand, you have probably drifted into Zone 3.

**You could hold this pace for an hour.** Zone 2 is a durability zone, not a speed zone. Sessions typically run 45 to 90 minutes because the whole point is time spent, not intensity reached.

**Heart rate should stay flat, not climb.** Some upward drift over a long session is normal, especially in heat. When heart rate rises by 15+ bpm at a constant pace, it usually indicates dehydration, heat stress, or an excessively high starting intensity.

**The formula is a floor, not a finish line.** A heart rate range tells you where to start. A lactate test or a properly calibrated talk test tells you where your real aerobic ceiling sits, because that ceiling is set by your mitochondria and muscle fiber makeup, not by your birth year.

## Zone 2 for Running, Cycling, and Walking

The heart rate math does not change by activity. The pace, wattage, or incline needed to hit that heart rate absolutely does.

- **Cycling:** Tends to produce a lower heart rate than running at a similar perceived effort, because running recruits more muscle mass and carries body weight against gravity.
- **Walking:** Can push beginners into their Zone 2 range on a slight incline alone. This is normal and not a sign of poor fitness — it means your aerobic engine has room to grow.
- **Running:** Requires discipline to run slow enough. If running puts you above Zone 2, alternate jogging and brisk walking.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is my Zone 2 heart rate?
It is roughly 60% to 70% of your estimated maximum heart rate. Using the Tanaka formula, subtract 0.7 times your age from 208 to get your max heart rate, then multiply that number by 0.60 and 0.70 to get your personal range.

### How do I calculate Zone 2 heart rate?
Calculate your max heart rate with $208 - (0.7 \times \text{age})$, then take 60% and 70% of that number. For a more personalized target, use the Karvonen formula, which includes your resting heart rate.

### How do I find my Zone 2 heart rate without a calculator?
Use the talk test. Exercise at a pace where you can speak in full sentences comfortably without gasping for breath. That intensity lines up with Zone 2 for most healthy adults.

### What is a good Zone 2 heart rate?
There is no universal "good" number, because it depends entirely on age and fitness level. During a good Zone 2 session, your heart rate remains within your target range for most of the workout, with breathing staying controlled throughout.

### How long should I train in Zone 2?
Most coaches recommend 45- to 90-minute sessions three to five times per week to achieve meaningful aerobic and mitochondrial adaptations. Shorter sessions still help, but consistency over months drives the biggest gains.

### What should Zone 2 feel like?
Comfortably easy, almost frustratingly so at first. You should be able to hold a conversation, breathe primarily through your nose, and finish the session feeling like you could have done more.

### Is Zone 2 the same for running and cycling?
The heart rate range remains identical because it is based on your physiology rather than your sport. What differs is the pace or power output needed to hit that heart rate.

### Is a Zone 2 heart rate calculator accurate for everyone?
It is a strong starting estimate for most healthy adults, with a typical margin of error of about 10 beats per minute. People on heart rate–affecting medication (like beta blockers) or diagnosed heart conditions should consult a physician.
