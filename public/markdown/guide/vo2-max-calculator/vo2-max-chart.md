## **How to Read This Chart**

Find your age bracket, then your sex, then locate where your number lands. That's your category, full stop: there's no need to overthink it further.

A few things worth keeping in mind while you do this:

Landing in "Average" isn't a bad result. It means you match the general population for your age and sex, most of whom aren't training their cardiovascular system on purpose. Moving up even one category, say, from Average to Good represents a real, measurable improvement in how your heart and lungs handle stress, and it's genuinely achievable with a few months of consistent aerobic training.

The categories also aren't evenly spaced. Notice how the gap between "Good" and "Excellent" tends to be smaller than the gap between "Poor" and "Fair" at most ages. That reflects how population data actually clusters: most people sit somewhere in the middle, and the tails thin out fast.

## **Why Scores Drop With Age**

Every bracket in both charts trends downward as age increases, and that's expected, not alarming. Maximum heart rate declines gradually starting in your late twenties, stroke volume (the amount of blood your heart pumps per beat) tends to shrink somewhat without training, and lean muscle mass naturally decreases with age unless you actively work to preserve it.

The encouraging part: this decline isn't fixed. Compare the "Excellent" range for a 60-year-old to the "Average" range for someone in their 30s, and you'll notice real overlap. A well-trained older adult can post a genuinely impressive number for their decade, sometimes matching untrained people 20 years younger. Age sets a general trend, not a hard ceiling.

## **A Note on Garmin, Apple Watch, and Other Wearable Charts**

If your Garmin or Apple Watch shows its own VO2 max chart or fitness age estimate, don't be surprised if it doesn't match this one exactly. Wearable brands often use proprietary scoring ranges calibrated to their user data rather than standard population studies. Treat the trend on your device as the useful signal: is your number climbing over the months rather than expecting the exact category labels to line up between sources.

## **Frequently Asked Questions**

**What is a good VO2 max for my age?** Check the tables above for your specific age bracket and sex. As a general rule, "Good" starts around the 70th percentile for your group, meaning you're fitter than most people your age.

**Why is the vo2 max chart different for men and women?** Men typically post higher scores at every age due to physiological differences, including larger average heart size, higher haemoglobin concentration, and greater lean muscle mass. This is a normal, expected gap, not a reflection of relative fitness effort.

**What is considered a superior VO2 max?** Superior generally starts around the top 5% for your age and sex bracket. Elite endurance athletes routinely score well above these charts entirely, sometimes reaching 70–90+ ml/kg/min.

**How much does VO2 max decline per decade?** On average, VO2 max drops by roughly 8–10% per decade after your late twenties for people who don't train specifically for cardio fitness. Regular aerobic exercise meaningfully slows this decline.

**Why does my fitness watch show a different VO2 max category than this chart?** Wearable brands use their own scoring algorithms and reference data, which can differ slightly from population studies like the ones used here. Focus on whether your number is trending upward over time rather than matching exact labels across sources.

**Can I move up a category on this chart?** Yes. Most untrained adults can raise their VO2 max by 15–20% within 8–12 weeks of consistent aerobic training, which is often enough to shift up a full category on the chart.
