Zone 2 training is a specific and measurable intensity of aerobic exercise, roughly 60–70% of your maximum heart rate. It is not a vibe. It has a number attached to it, and that number is what makes it useful.

This guide breaks down the definition, how it compares to the zones, and answers the practical questions people actually type into Google before their next run.

## **The Definition**

Zone 2 training sits just below your first lactate threshold (LT1), the point where lactate starts building up in your blood faster than your body clears it. Below that line, your muscles run mostly on fat for fuel, and your effort feels sustainable for a long time. Cross it, and the clock on your endurance starts ticking down fast.

In heart rate terms, that translates to roughly 60% to 70% of your estimated maximum heart rate for most healthy adults. In feel terms, it is the pace where you can hold a full conversation without gasping for air between sentences.

## **Zone 1 vs Zone 2 vs Zone 3**

Heart rate zones exist on a spectrum, and Zone 2 only makes sense next to its neighbors.

| Zone 1 | 50–60% | Very easy, recovery pace | Active recovery, warm-up |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Zone 2 | 60–70% | Comfortably hard, conversational | Aerobic base, endurance |
| Zone 3 | 70–80% | Moderately hard, "grey zone" | Tempo work, often overused by accident |

Zone 3 has a reputation problem in endurance circles, and it earned it fairly. It is hard enough to build fatigue but not hard enough to trigger the strongest adaptations of real high-intensity work. Most people who think they are training in Zone 2 are actually camped out in Zone 3, working harder than they need to for the aerobic benefit they are after.

## **How to Find Your Zone 2 Heart Rate**

You have two reliable options.

**The formula method:** Estimate your maximum heart rate using the Tanaka formula: (208 \- (0.7 × your age). Multiply that resulting number by 0.60 and 0.70 to determine your personal target range.

**The talk test:** Exercise at a pace where you can speak in complete sentences but would rather not sing along to a song. That intensity lines up with Zone 2 for the vast majority of people, no math required.

Because maximum heart rate declines over time, your Zone 2 range shifts with age. Consequently, a 25-year-old trains at a noticeably higher target than a 60-year-old, despite experiencing the same level of perceived exertion.

## **Zone 2 Training Examples**

Zone 2 is not a single workout. It is an intensity you can apply to almost any steady-state cardio.

* **Zone 2 running:** An easy jog featuring controlled breathing and a pace that feels almost too slow to count. Many runners must slow down significantly when first training by heart rate rather than pace.  
* **Zone 2 cycling:** A steady spin on flat terrain or a moderate resistance on a stationary bike, held for 45 to 90 minutes.  
* **Zone 2 walking:** A brisk walk, often on an incline, which pushes beginners into their target heart rate range without any running involved at all.  
* Rowing, swimming, and hiking all work the same way. The activity changes; the heart rate target does not.

## **Is Zone 2 Aerobic? Does It Burn Fat?**

Yes to both, with some nuance worth knowing.

Zone 2 is aerobic by definition. It relies on oxygen to produce energy, unlike the anaerobic pathways used during higher intensities. At this output, the body draws a larger share of fuel from fat, which is where the "fat-burning zone" label originates.

This label is slightly misleading. Because the caloric burn per minute is lower in Zone 2 than in intense training, a longer session is required to match the total output of a shorter, harder workout. Rather than rapid weight loss, the primary benefits of Zone 2 fat oxidation lie in improving metabolic health and managing insulin resistance.

## **Is Zone 2 Training a Waste of Time?**

No, but it is not the singular miracle some corners of the internet make it out to be either.

A 2025 narrative review in *Sports Medicine* found that current evidence does not support Zone 2 as the optimal intensity for building mitochondrial capacity, the claim most responsible for its viral popularity. Higher-intensity training tends to drive faster, larger gains in mitochondrial function and VO2max, especially when weekly training time is limited.

## **Building a Basic Zone 2 Training Plan**

Start with two to three 30- to 45-minute sessions per week, increasing duration before adding frequency. Beginners often realize meaningful gains from consistent Zone 2 work. Pair it with a few higher-intensity sessions for a program that actually drives results.
