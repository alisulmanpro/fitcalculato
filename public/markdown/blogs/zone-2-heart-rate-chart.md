## **The Full Heart Rate Zone Chart**

Every heart rate zone system relies on the same core principle: a percentage of your maximum heart rate. Five zones is the standard most coaches, wearables, and sports scientists use.

| Zone 1 | 50–60% | Very light, easy breathing | Recovery, warm-up, cool-down |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Zone 2** | **60–70%** | **Comfortable, conversational** | **Aerobic base, fat metabolism, endurance** |
| Zone 3 | 70–80% | Moderately hard, borderline breathless | Tempo work, often overused by accident |
| Zone 4 | 80–90% | Hard, short sentences only | Lactate threshold, race-pace effort |
| Zone 5 | 90–100% | Maximal, unsustainable | Sprints, VO2 max intervals |

Zone 2 sits right in the middle of the "easy" end of the chart, but it is not Zone 1\. The difference matters: Zone 1 is closer to true rest, while Zone 2 is the highest intensity you can hold before your body starts leaning harder on carbohydrate over fat for fuel.

As your max heart rate naturally declines over time, your Zone 2 range shifts with it. Here is a condensed version using the Tanaka formula, 208 − (0.7 × age).

## **Zone 2 Heart Rate Chart by Resting Heart Rate (Karvonen Method)**

Age alone does not capture fitness level. Two 40-year-olds can have genuinely different Zone 2 ranges depending on how fit their heart already is. The Karvonen formula factors in resting heart rate to personalize the number.

For a 40-year-old (max HR of 180), here is how the Zone 2 range shifts based on resting heart rate:

| 50 bpm | 115–128 bpm |
| :---- | :---- |
| 60 bpm | 120–132 bpm |
| 70 bpm | 125–136 bpm |
| 80 bpm | 130–140 bpm |

Notice the pattern: a lower resting heart rate, usually a sign of better cardiovascular fitness, pushes the Zone 2 range slightly lower too. Fitter hearts do more work per beat, so they need fewer beats per minute to hit the same effort level.

## **How to Actually Use This Chart**

A chart on a screen does not train you. Here is how to put it to work.

**Pick your method first:** If you only know your age, use the age-based chart. If you have a resting heart rate from a fitness tracker, use the Karvonen numbers for more precision.

**Give yourself a buffer, not a single number:** Aim to stay inside the full range rather than chasing one exact bpm. Heart rate naturally drifts a few beats during a session, especially in heat.

**Recheck the chart yearly:** Maximum heart rate shifts gradually with age, and resting heart rate shifts with fitness level. A chart from three years ago may already be a few beats off.

**Use it alongside the talk test:** If your heart rate says Zone 2 but you cannot speak in full sentences, trust your breathing over the monitor. Wearable heart rate sensors, especially wrist-based ones, can read a few beats high or low during fast-paced movement.

## **FAQs**

**What heart rate is Zone 2 on this chart?** Zone 2 sits at 60% to 70% of your maximum heart rate, or roughly 50% to 60% of your heart rate reserve if you are using the Karvonen method with resting heart rate.

**Is the Zone 2 heart rate chart the same for running and cycling?**  No. Your heart rate percentages stay the same regardless of activity because they reflect your cardiovascular system, not the sport. What differs is the pace or output needed to reach that heart rate.

**Why do different apps show different Zone 2 numbers for me?** Most fitness apps default to either the Fox formula (220 − age) or the Tanaka formula (208 − 0.7 × age), and some use Karvonen if they have your resting heart rate on file. Small differences between apps usually come down to which formula they picked, not an error.
